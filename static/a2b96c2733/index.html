Why We're Doing This

Since quietly dropping its pledge not to build AI-powered weapons or mass surveillance, Google has doubled down on unethical applications of the technology. Here are just some of the things it has been up to.

Google Erased Its Own Ban on AI for Weapons and Surveillance

Weeks into Trump's second term, Google quietly deleted its 2018 pledge never to build AI weapons or rights-violating surveillance, made after staff protests over a military drone contract. It wasn't framed as a reversal: the sole edit note read, 'We've made updates to our AI Principles.'

Google's Own Lawyers Warned Its AI Could Enable Human Rights Abuses. It Signed Anyway.

Google's own lawyers and consultants warned that providing AI to the Israeli military 'could be used for, or linked to, the facilitation of human rights violations,' and urged withholding the technology entirely. Google signed the $1.2bn deal anyway, keeping no reliable way to monitor how it was used.

Israel Used Google Photos to Build a Gaza 'Hit List'

An Israeli official said Google Photos' face-matching 'worked better' than purpose-built tools in Israel's Gaza-wide facial-recognition dragnet, helping build a 'hit list'. Thousands were detained, some tortured. Asked to explain, Google declined.

Google Is Helping the Trump Administration Deploy AI Along the Mexican Border

Border surveillance towers in Arizona are getting AI that spots people and vehicles automatically, with the video analysed on CBP's Google Cloud. In 2020, Google Cloud's CEO told staff the company was 'not working on any projects associated with immigration enforcement at the southern border.'

Google's GenAI in Lockheed Martin's AI Factory

Lockheed Martin is a US aerospace and defence contractor whose main customer is the US Department of War. Google announced a genAI partnership with its AI Factory in 'secure, on-premises, and air-gapped environments': unclassified systems first, then classified ones.

Who We Are

We are FTEs from across all units, functions and levels in GDM UK. DeepMind was founded on ethical principles, committed to building AI for the benefit of all humanity. We want to honour that original commitment.

We believe AI has the potential to benefit society. But technological progress doesn’t automatically translate into shared prosperity: Nobel laureates Acemoglu and Johnson argue that organised workers were critical in turning post-war progress into prosperity for all.

Our priorities and demands are decided by employees like you through a democratic process. We are getting legal and negotiation support from our union's national office, but everything else is done by GDMers for GDMers.

We'd love you to join us! →

Today, society has little say over AI. A handful of private companies control almost all of the compute infrastructure, and they decide where and how AI is deployed. That gives them global influence beyond democratic accountability, and regulation is struggling to keep up.

Google and other AI companies are signing more and more military and surveillance contracts, with little regard for international law and human rights. These contracts often place no limits on how the technology is used while leaving companies with little visibility into what customers do: Nimbus and the Department of War contracts are clear examples. This is happening as models grow more powerful and safety incidents multiply.

Meanwhile, AI’s energy demands are pulling companies away from their climate commitments: Google’s own 2026 Environmental Report admits its “AI infrastructure buildout is currently accelerating faster than the grid is decarbonizing”.

With a recognised union, we get a say in how our work is used and act as a counterweight to corporate power, helping steer the technology we build onto a better path. In this, we follow generations of workers who stood up for ethical uses of their work. As union members, we are also consulted by regulators, Parliament and MPs, so we can contribute with our expertise to help shape future policies.

Enforceable Principles

Google must publicly define and firmly uphold red lines around impermissible applications of its AI systems, and always be true to its commitment to human rights and international law. These red lines are only meaningful if backed by enforceable safeguards and persistent monitoring.

Enforceable Principles

  • 1.1 No contracts with partners accused of human rights violations, unless an independent third-party assessment, publicly disclosed, verifies the technology will not be used to facilitate such violations.

    This responsibility follows from the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights, which Alphabet has long committed to in its own statement on human rights. The United Nations, Amnesty International, and Alphabet shareholders have all identified contracts upon which the UNGPs should compel Alphabet to act.

  • 1.2 Restore red lines on harmful applications (surveillance, weapons).

    We will not pursue weapons, technologies or contracts whose principal purpose, implementation or impact causes harm or directly facilitates injury to people, or gather or use information for surveillance violating internationally accepted norms.

  • 1.3 AI principles in contract terms. AI principles must be a non-negotiable part of any contract and apply equally whether we build the whole system or just supply an AI component in someone else's pipeline.

  • 1.4 AI principles must be durable, with a transparent, participatory process to change them.

  • 1.5 AI principles must be enforced and monitored, with independent oversight.

    AI principles must be written in a way that makes them clear, specific, understandable, and enforceable. Google should then apply robust monitoring mechanisms that can detect almost any usage of our systems that violates the principles, and monitoring capabilities should be preserved in the deployment arrangements.

    Independent oversight should complement internal monitoring and enforcement, so that a party other than Google can verify principles are upheld in practice. If any violation is found, Google should immediately apply effective enforcement measures that cause this misuse to cease.

  • 1.6 Google must create accountability for violations, with real consequences.

    Within the company, Google must lay out meaningful, proportionate consequences for workers who take actions that violate our AI principles. These consequences must apply to any Googler at any level, without exceptions.

Transparency & Right to Abstain

Public trust and effective oversight of AI depend on transparency. Google should disclose how its AI is deployed, the safeguards in place and the human rights due diligence conducted, and any GDMer should be able to decline unethical work without retaliation.

Transparency & Right to Abstain

  • 2.1 Publish policies on client and deployment selection. Google must publish the policies we apply to determine whether we can ethically do business with a client, in accordance with our principles, and the policies we apply before, during, and after the time when we supply our technology to the client.

  • 2.2 Publicly report on business with governments, defence, law-enforcement and intelligence agencies.

    On a regular basis, Google must issue public reports showing summary statistics like the number of these engagements and their overall value, broken down by the categories listed above and also by country. Relations routed through vendors or subcontractors should be included.

    Reports should also provide information about human rights due diligence conducted, the permitted uses and deployment arrangements, monitoring capabilities and safeguards governing deployment. They should also specify any terms requiring Google to modify, disable, or remove safety measures at the customer's request.

  • 2.3 Right to abstain.

    Workers at GDM must have the right to decline to work on projects that they believe violate the ethical commitments that Google and GDM have made, without fear of retaliation or professional impact. This right applies to any GDMer at any level, without exceptions.

The recognition process will proceed as follows:

  1. Step 1

    CAC accepts the application

    The CAC will check that the bargaining unit (BU, the employees we represent) is appropriate and that we have enough members (at least 10%). If so, it will accept the application. Our BU is Google DeepMind’s product area (PA).

  2. Step 2

    Ballot

    Everyone in the bargaining unit, members and non-members alike, is called to vote on whether they want our unions to be recognised. The ballot will likely happen via postal vote. Commit to vote yes in the recognition ballot.

  3. Step 3

    Result

    If a majority of those who vote say yes, our unions are recognised. There is no minimum turnout.

  4. Step 4

    Bargaining

    By law, statutory recognition covers pay, hours and holidays. This is just the legal minimum. We will seek to extend it to ethics and transparency through the recognition agreement, then negotiate a collective agreement on these issues.

FAQ

Joining

Can I stay anonymous?

Yes.

Your employer does not have a legal right to know if you are a member of a trade union. Unions keep their membership lists strictly confidential. Under Data Protection (UK GDPR), trade union membership is classified as "special category data", meaning it requires the highest level of privacy protection.

If the union pursues statutory recognition through the UK Government's Central Arbitration Committee (CAC), the CAC will need to verify the union has sufficient membership within the bargaining unit — typically at least 10% of workers. To do this, membership data may need to be submitted to the CAC, which is an independent governmental body.

Critically, this data is not shared with Google. The CAC handles it confidentially, and the employer only sees the membership percentage.

The law forbids employers from compiling lists of union members — see Blacklisting Regulations 2010, Reg. 3.

Who can join?

Membership is open to all UK employees of Google, including TVC. This specific recognition campaign, however, only covers Google DeepMind FTEs. We encourage people outside of Google DeepMind to join UTAW anyway: we all work together, and in the future we can try to get recognition in other bargaining units.

Unfortunately, colleagues based outside the UK (for example in the US) aren't eligible. There are other options:

  • Join the Coalition of Concerned AI Staff, an international, inter-lab effort sharing growing concerns about how AI is being developed and used in the real world, with a focus on military and surveillance applications.
  • Join an international coalition of Google DeepMind employees: AIOC.
  • Join No Tech for Apartheid, a worker-led labour organisation of Google workers organising to hold Google accountable for creating positive impact in the world and challenging complicity in militarism and surveillance.
  • US colleagues can organise with our sister union, the Alphabet Workers Union (AWU).
How much does it cost, and what do I get?

Membership is £8.70 a month, much lower than many unions (Alphabet Workers Union members, for example, pay 1% of total compensation). This covers the cost of running the union — trained organisers, legal advice, and support when you need it.

In return you get:

  • Access to our free legal helpline and advice from day one, and training on your rights.
  • Individual representation in disciplinary and grievance hearings (for issues arising after your first eight weeks of membership), so it's worth joining before you need it.
  • Everyday member discounts that alone can more than cover the cost: discounted supermarket vouchers, free cinema tickets, cheaper gym memberships, and savings on holidays, travel, insurance and more.

We follow a one-member, one-vote principle. By joining, you can help shape our demands. You can also be involved in our talks with policymakers and help give form to AI regulation.

What legal protection are we entitled to?
  • Protection from dismissal for union activity — Section 152 TULRCA 1992
  • Protection from detriment (demotion, pay cut, harassment) for union activity — Section 146 TULRCA 1992
  • Protection from dismissal for taking part in lawful industrial action (now automatically unfair, no qualifying period) — Section 238A TULRCA, as amended by Employment Rights Act 2025
  • Protection from detriment for industrial action (coming into force October 2026) — Employment Rights Act 2025, s.76 (which inserts s.236A into TULRCA 1992)
  • Right to paid time off for union representatives to carry out duties — Section 168 TULRCA 1992
  • Right to unpaid time off for all members to take part in union activities — Section 170 TULRCA 1992
  • Individual representation in disciplinary and grievance hearings — Employment Relations Act 1999, s.10
  • Legal support through the union if things go wrong — Union rulebook / membership agreement
  • Democratic voice in setting demands and balloting on action — Section 226 TULRCA 1992 (ballot provisions)
  • Protection from blacklisting based on union activities — Blacklisting Regulations 2010

Recognition

What is union recognition?

Recognition is what gives your union the legal right to negotiate with the employer on workers' behalf. Without recognition, the employer can simply ignore your union. With recognition, they are legally obliged to engage. By securing union recognition, workers gain formal rights to collective bargaining, disclosure of internal company information, and mandatory consultation on major workplace changes.

When a union applies for recognition, it defines the bargaining unit, which is the group of workers that it seeks to represent. For example, it can be all of Google DeepMind, or a smaller unit. Recognition can happen in two ways: voluntary or statutory.

The recognition process ends with a recognition agreement.

What is voluntary recognition?

The employer simply agrees to work with the union in the scope defined by the recognition agreement. For voluntary recognition to happen, at least 10% of the bargaining unit must be members, and the majority of members should vote in favour of the recognition. The vote is internal to the union.

We asked for voluntary recognition on 4 May, but Google refused. Negotiations via ACAS also failed, so we are now applying for statutory recognition.

What is statutory recognition?

If an employer refuses to recognise a union, the union can apply to the Central Arbitration Committee (CAC). The CAC will verify whether a minimum membership threshold is met (10% of the bargaining unit), and then all employees in the unit are called to vote (ballot). A majority vote in favour is sufficient for the recognition to happen.

Statutory recognition covers pay, hours and holidays by law; see how we can extend it to other topics.

What is a recognition agreement?

A recognition agreement is a formal document which sets out who is covered by the union (the bargaining unit), what issues can be negotiated, how bargaining meetings work, what facilities and time off union representatives are entitled to, and how disputes are resolved if the two sides cannot agree.

It sets the rules for bargaining; what is actually agreed goes into collective bargaining agreements.

A recognition agreement is not set in stone. It can be expanded, updated, or renegotiated over time, and doing so is often one of the union's most important long-term goals.

What happens if recognition fails?

The union will not be able to apply for statutory recognition in the same or a similar bargaining unit for three years after a failed process. It can apply after one year in a different bargaining unit. Voluntary recognition can happen at any time.

Bargaining & demands

What demands can be discussed at the bargaining table? How can we extend beyond pay / hours / holidays?

Where a union gains recognition through the statutory process, it has the legal right to bargain collectively over pay, hours, and holiday entitlement. These are the minimum issues covered by the recognition agreement, but there is no restriction on topics we can discuss in collective bargaining — including business practices, company priorities and governance. To do so, we need to pressure Google to extend the recognition agreement. Our industrial leverage includes actions short of a strike, campaigns, and ultimately strikes if the employer refuses. The stronger the union, the more effective the pressure. Most often, a credible threat is all it takes. Getting recognition is just the first step — then we work on extending the recognition agreement.

How are demands agreed upon?

Demands are set through a democratic process: they come from the membership. This is what gives them legitimacy, both within the union and in the eyes of the employer.

  1. Consultation: members are asked about their priorities through meetings, surveys, or conversations with union representatives. This is where the issues that matter most to workers are identified and collected.
  2. Drafting: union representatives collate the feedback and draft a set of demands that reflects the membership's priorities.
  3. Approval: members vote on or approve the demands before they are formally presented to the employer.

After approval, collective bargaining begins. This is where the demands are negotiated with the employer until a deal is reached.

Collective bargaining is an ongoing process, not a one-off event. New demands can be raised, and existing ones amended, at any time through the same democratic process.

What is a collective bargaining agreement?

A collective bargaining agreement (or collective agreement) is the deal reached between the union and the employer through bargaining. The recognition agreement defines the topics that can be negotiated. The union puts forward its demands, and the collective agreement is what is agreed upon after negotiation.

Bargaining is ongoing, so collective agreements are renegotiated over time: each round can produce a new or updated one.

How does a bargaining process end?
  • A deal is reached: members need to vote to approve it, and it becomes a collective bargaining agreement. They can also reject it and instruct union representatives to return to the table.
  • A deal is not reached: members can be balloted on whether to escalate, including through industrial action.

General

Why doesn't Google want us to form a union?

Together we can formally act as an employee-side counterweight to management's ability to exercise unchecked authority. Management's self-interest is to prevent us from having this kind of power.

I don't want to be affected by decisions made by union members — what can I do?

Decisions that affect you are already made by a much smaller pool of management executives, with no visibility or meaningful input from you: you were not consulted on the removal of the autonomous weapons or mass surveillance pledges from the AI Principles, nor on safety risks from contracts with significant human rights implications.

Collective decision-making improves democratic power rather than reducing it. We will have a way to demand changes, and the company will have to consult us before making major decisions. Surveys can be run among all employees to better understand concerns. You will never be forced to engage with or join a union.

Have you tried internal channels, for example the UK Googler Forums?

We have tried to open dialogue through the UK Googler Forums, but leadership declined to discuss ethical matters through this forum. Other routes — petitions with broad support, letters, questions at town halls, even protected disclosures — have also been tried and ignored.
